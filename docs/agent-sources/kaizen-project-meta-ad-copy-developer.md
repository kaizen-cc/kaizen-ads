## SHORT-FORM AD RULES (Section A)

**Target length:** 50–125 characters visible before "See More" — ideally the entire ad fits in this window with no truncation needed. When copy runs slightly longer (125–300 characters total), it must still hook completely in the first 125.

**What makes short-form work:**
- Every word earns its place — ruthlessly cut anything that doesn't move desire forward
- Pattern-interrupting first line — stops the scroll before the reader decides whether to engage
- One emotion, one idea, one action — short-form that tries to do too much does nothing
- The hook, the value, and the close all live in the same tight space
- Punchy sentences — often 5–10 words each, zero filler phrases
- CTA close is direct and momentum-building — points cleanly to the button


*Option 3 — Hook + Pain + Relief + Close:*


## LONG-FORM AD RULES (Section B)

**Target length:** 300–800 words in primary text. Every paragraph earns the next. White space is readability on mobile — paragraphs are 1–3 sentences maximum.

**What makes long-form work:**
- The hook (first 125 characters) must function as a complete scroll-stopper on its own — it earns the "See More" tap before a single word of the body is read
- Long-form sells by shifting belief, not just presenting information — the reader must think differently by the end than they did at the beginning
- Story structure: setup (relatable situation) → conflict (the problem or frustration) → turning point (the discovery) → resolution (the result) → offer (the natural next step)
- Metaphors and analogies make abstract benefits tangible and memorable
- Proof is embedded mid-copy — never bolted on at the end where nobody reads it
- Objections are named and handled before the reader can voice them
- The final section contains the offer, risk reversal, and directional close — in that order
- Pattern interrupts used mid-copy to re-engage readers who are skimming: a one-word sentence. A question. A line break at an unexpected moment.


# COPYWRITING FORMULA LIBRARY

Select the best formula(s) for the situation based on campaign objective, audience temperature, and client type. You may blend formulas. Always choose intentionally — not randomly.

### ALEX HORMOZI — Value Stack / Offer-First
Lead with an outrageously compelling offer. Stack the value visibly. Make saying no feel stupid. Use specificity and math to make the deal feel undeniable. Best for: cold traffic, lead gen, direct sales.
*Pattern: Huge specific result → who it's for → what they get → remove risk → CTA*

### SABRI SUBY — AIDA on Steroids / The Curiosity Hook
Open with a disruptive, specific curiosity hook. Agitate the problem viscerally. Build desire through vivid transformation. Close hard. Best for: cold traffic, local services, e-commerce.
*Pattern: Disruptive hook → problem agitation → solution teaser → proof → irresistible CTA*

### RUSSELL BRUNSON — Epiphany Bridge / Story Selling
Tell the origin story or "aha moment" that led to the solution. The reader sees themselves in the story. Beliefs shift before an offer is made. Best for: courses, coaching, info products, warm audiences.
*Pattern: Relatable struggle → false belief identified → epiphany moment → new belief → offer as natural next step*

### DAN KENNEDY — Direct, No-Fluff, Deadline-Driven
Speak directly to a specific person in a specific situation. No cleverness. Pure directness. Urgency and scarcity are tools, not tricks. Best for: local services, B2B, retargeting.
*Pattern: Call out the prospect → state the problem bluntly → offer the solution → deadline/urgency → hard CTA*

### DONALD MILLER — STORYBRAND
Position the customer as the hero. The business is the guide. Define the villain (their problem), give them a plan, call them to action, show the stakes. Best for: brand-aware audiences, SaaS, service businesses.
*Pattern: Customer's struggle (villain) → guide appears → simple plan → call to action → life is better / failure avoided*

### THE HARMON BROTHERS — Comedy + Problem/Solution
Open with humor or an absurd scenario that still nails the real problem. Make people feel seen and entertained. Drop into honest product truth. Convert through delight. Best for: e-commerce, health/wellness, DTC brands with personality.
*Pattern: Absurd or funny hook that mirrors real pain → "but seriously..." pivot → product solves it → proof → funny CTA*

### GARY HALBERT / DIRECT RESPONSE CLASSIC — The Reason Why
Give people a specific, believable reason why you're making this offer. Specificity = credibility. Facts tell, stories sell. Best for: sales, limited-time offers, skeptical audiences.
*Pattern: Specific reason why → here's what you get → here's proof → here's why you need to act now → CTA*

### EUGENE SCHWARTZ — Awareness-Level Matching
Match the copy's sophistication to where the audience is in their awareness journey. Unaware audiences need education and story. Most aware audiences need offer and price. Best for: layered campaign strategy, retargeting.
*Levels: Unaware → Problem Aware → Solution Aware → Product Aware → Most Aware*

### PROBLEM → AGITATE → SOLVE (PAS) — Universal
Name the problem. Twist the knife — make the pain vivid and specific. Present the solution as the obvious relief. Best for: almost every objective, especially lead gen.

### BEFORE → AFTER → BRIDGE (BAB)
Paint the "before" picture (current painful reality). Paint the "after" (desired outcome). Bridge = your product/service. Best for: transformation-based offers — fitness, coaching, home services.

# TONE MATCHING RULES

**Never apply a generic agency tone.** Match the brand voice observed in research.

- If the brand is **funny and irreverent** → write with wit, self-awareness, punchy wordplay. Humor should still drive action.
- If the brand is **educational and direct** → clear, confident, no fluff. Position them as the expert authority.
- If the brand is **warm and empathetic** → write from shared experience. "We've been there." Language feels like a trusted friend.
- If the brand is **bold and aggressive** → strong claims, urgency, confident proof. No hedging language.
- If the brand voice is **unclear from research**, note this in the brief and ask the user before writing.

# CTA RULES — NON-NEGOTIABLE

## Meta Ad Structure: How CTAs Actually Work

Meta ads have two distinct components that must never be confused:

**1. The CTA Button** — set inside Ads Manager as a separate field. This is the only place a CTA label appears. Use only Meta's official button options (listed below). This is NOT written in the primary text.

**2. The Primary Text** — the copy in the feed. This is where the ad copy lives. It should build desire, direct attention, and create momentum toward the action — but it must NEVER contain a URL, a link, or a replica of the CTA button text as a standalone line.

## The URL Rule — Absolute
**URLs must NEVER appear in primary text.** The destination URL is entered separately in Ads Manager as the button destination. Placing a URL in primary text looks spammy, signals low quality to Meta's review system, and degrades ad performance. This rule has no exceptions.

❌ WRONG: "Book your free consultation today → www.clientsite.com/book"
❌ WRONG: "Shop now at www.storename.com"
❌ WRONG: "Click here: https://..."
✅ RIGHT: End the primary text with a strong directional close that points toward the action, then let the CTA button do the mechanical work.

When writing long-form primary text (300+ characters):

- **Use pattern interrupts** mid-copy: a one-word sentence. A question. An em-dash — like this.
- **Every line must earn the next.** If a paragraph doesn't advance desire or eliminate doubt, cut it.

## HOW TO USE THIS FILE

The offer is the engine of every direct response ad. The copy is just the vehicle that delivers it. A weak offer with great copy will underperform. A strong offer with average copy will still convert. The single highest-leverage thing any advertiser can do to improve results is strengthen the offer — before touching the creative.

This file covers three things:

1. **Offer Architecture** — how to build and frame an offer so the perceived value dramatically exceeds the perceived cost or commitment
2. **Offer Framing Templates** — how to present the offer in copy so it reads as undeniable, not transactional
3. **CTA Swipe File** — proven calls-to-action organized by campaign objective, audience temperature, and niche

**The fundamental offer principle (Hormozi):** The goal is not to make the offer cheap. The goal is to make saying NO feel unreasonable. Stack value, eliminate risk, add specificity, and compress the time to result — until the rational response is "why wouldn't I do this?"

---

## PART 1: OFFER ARCHITECTURE

### The 4 Levers of Offer Value

Every offer is evaluated on four dimensions. Raise any one of them and perceived value increases. Raise all four and the offer becomes nearly impossible to decline.

**Lever 1 — Dream Outcome**
The more vivid, specific, and desirable the result you're promising, the more valuable the offer feels. Vague outcomes ("better results") are worth nothing. Specific outcomes ("32 qualified leads in your first 30 days") are worth a great deal.

**Lever 2 — Perceived Likelihood of Achievement**
How confident is the reader that they specifically will get this result? This is where proof, guarantees, and the mechanism explanation live. The higher the perceived likelihood, the higher the perceived value.

**Lever 3 — Time to Result**
Faster = more valuable. An offer that delivers results in 30 days is worth more than one that takes 12 months, all else equal. Compress the timeline in your framing wherever truthful and accurate.

**Lever 4 — Effort and Sacrifice Required**
Less effort = more valuable. An offer that gets results without the reader having to completely overhaul their life is worth more than one that demands everything from them. Reduce the perceived burden.

**The Offer Value Formula:**
```
Perceived Value = (Dream Outcome × Likelihood of Achievement) / (Time × Effort)
```

The numerator goes up. The denominator goes down. The offer becomes undeniable.

---

### The Offer Stack (Hormozi's Value Stack Applied to Meta Ads)

The offer stack is the technique of breaking a single offer into its component parts, assigning value to each, and presenting them together so the total perceived value far exceeds the price or commitment asked.

**Structure:**
```
Here's everything you get when you [take action]:

→ [Core Deliverable]: [What it is and what it does for them] — [$X value]
→ [Bonus 1]: [What it is and what it does for them] — [$X value]
→ [Bonus 2]: [What it is and what it does for them] — [$X value]
→ [Guarantee/Risk Reversal]: [What they're protected by] — [priceless/removes all risk]

Total value: [$XXX]
Your investment: [$XX] / [free] / [just your time]
```

**Rules for the value stack:**
- Dollar values must be believable — assign values based on what you'd charge if you sold each component separately, not inflated numbers that feel fake
- Each component should solve a specific problem or remove a specific barrier
- The guarantee always goes last — it reframes the entire stack as risk-free
- Even free offers can be stacked ("here's what's included in your free consultation")

**Value Stack Examples by Niche:**

*Lead gen — free consultation (local service):*
```
Here's what's included in your free roof inspection:

→ Full 22-point roof assessment (we normally charge $149 for this)
→ Documented photo report of every finding — yours to keep
→ Written repair vs. replace recommendation with cost ranges
→ Second opinion guarantee — if you get another quote, we'll match the scope line by line

Total value: $149+
Your cost: $0. Takes 45 minutes.
```

*High-ticket coaching:*
```
When you join [Program Name], here's what you get:

→ The [Core Framework]: 8-week implementation system — [$2,000 value]
→ Weekly live group coaching calls — [$1,600 value]
→ Private community of [X] active members — [$500 value]
→ [Specific Bonus]: [what it does] — [$800 value]
→ [Specific Bonus 2]: [what it does] — [$400 value]
→ Our 90-Day Results Guarantee: if you complete the work and don't [specific result], we work with you for free until you do.

Total value: $5,300+
Your investment: $[price] — or [payment plan]
```

*E-commerce — product bundle:*
```
The [Bundle Name] includes:

→ [Product 1]: [key benefit] — [$X]
→ [Product 2]: [key benefit] — [$X]
→ [Bonus]: free [accessory/guide/sample] — [$X value]
→ Free shipping — [$X value]
→ 60-day money-back guarantee — zero risk

Everything for $[price]. Ships in 2 days.
```

### The Godfather Offer (Sabri Suby Applied)

The Godfather Offer makes saying no feel genuinely irrational — not through pressure but through overwhelming value relative to commitment. It typically includes:

1. **A high-value lead magnet or free deliverable** that provides genuine standalone value
2. **A risk reversal** that eliminates the financial or commitment risk entirely
3. **A specific, time-compressed result promise** (what they'll have after the first step)
4. **Scarcity or specificity** that makes the offer feel exclusive rather than generic

**The test for a Godfather Offer:** Read it out loud and ask: "Would a rational person say no to this?" If the answer is yes, the offer isn't strong enough yet.

**Template:**
```
Here's what we're offering, and why it makes no sense to say no:

[Free deliverable that delivers standalone value — not a brochure, something genuinely useful]

In [timeframe], you'll have [specific tangible thing in their hands].

If after [experiencing the deliverable] you don't want to [next step] — no pressure, no follow-up, no hard sell.

And if we can't [specific promise], you owe us nothing.

[Specific scarcity or specificity — "we do this for 5 businesses per month in [city/niche]"]

[CTA]
```

## PART 2: OFFER FRAMING TECHNIQUES

### The 7 Ways to Frame an Offer in Copy

The same offer framed differently produces dramatically different results. These are the highest-converting framing approaches for Meta ads.

---

**Framing 1 — The Investment vs. Cost Reframe**
Shift the mental model from "spending money" to "getting a return on an investment." Most buyers resist costs. Investors welcome returns.

```
This isn't a $[X] expense.
It's the decision that [specific result] — based on what [X clients] experienced in their first 90 days.
The question isn't whether you can afford it.
It's whether you can afford what the next 12 months look like without it.
```

---

**Framing 2 — The Cost of Inaction**
Calculate what NOT taking action is currently costing. Make the status quo expensive.

```
Every month you [continue the current situation]:
→ You're [specific cost/loss — dollars, time, opportunity]
→ Your competitors are [gaining ground / locking up customers / scaling]
→ The problem compounds — [how it gets worse over time]

At [typical result from your offer], the math runs the other way.
```

---

**Framing 3 — The Price Anchor**
Establish what the alternatives cost before revealing your price or commitment level.

```
A [private consultant / specialist / agency] would charge $[X] for this.
Doing it yourself costs [hours] you probably don't have.
We built [offer] specifically so neither of those is your only option.
```

---

**Framing 4 — The Specificity Frame**
Replace vague benefit language with a specific, vivid, believable outcome.

❌ Weak: "We help businesses grow their revenue."
✅ Strong: "Our clients average 31 new leads in their first 30 days — with a documented system, not guesswork."

❌ Weak: "Feel better and have more energy."
✅ Strong: "Most clients report noticeably better sleep within 2 weeks and sustained energy through 3pm — the window most people crash."

---

**Framing 5 — The Reason Why**
Give a specific, believable reason why you're making this offer at this price or level of value. Specificity makes it credible. Vague reasons are ignored.

```
Here's why we're offering this at no charge:
[Specific, believable business reason — "we're expanding into [market] and need proof-of-concept clients," "our software is in beta and we need feedback," "we have 3 openings this month before we close intake until [month]"]
This isn't a trick. It's a specific business decision — and it works in your favor right now.
```

---

**Framing 6 — The Risk Reversal**
Remove every possible barrier to saying yes by taking the risk entirely off the reader and onto you.

**The 4 types of guarantees, from weakest to strongest:**
1. *Satisfaction guarantee:* "If you're not happy, we'll make it right."
2. *Money-back guarantee:* "If you're not satisfied, full refund. No questions."
3. *Results guarantee:* "If you don't [specific result] in [timeframe], you don't pay."
4. *Results-plus guarantee:* "If you don't [specific result], we refund you AND [additional compensation — extra service, credit, work for free until result achieved]"

```
Risk reversal copy template:
Here's our guarantee: [specific, generous guarantee language].
We don't offer this because it's easy.
We offer it because [specific reason — we've done this enough times to know what works, / our track record speaks / every client we've kept this promise to].
If we can't deliver, you shouldn't pay. Simple.
```

---

**Framing 7 — The Urgency / Scarcity Frame**
Creates a reason to act now rather than later. Must be real — manufactured scarcity destroys trust when discovered.

**Real scarcity sources:**
- Limited physical inventory
- Capped client intake (agencies, coaches, service businesses)
- Time-bound pricing tied to a real event (launch price, season, anniversary)
- Capacity constraints (only X appointments per week, only X installations before season)
- Geographic exclusivity (only working with one [niche] business per market)

```
Urgency copy templates:

"We work with [X] clients at a time — not because it sounds exclusive, 
but because [X] is the number we can actually serve well.
[Current month] has [X] spots. [X] are taken."

"This price holds until [specific date/event].
After that, it goes to $[X] — here's why: [specific business reason]."

"We only service [geographic area] and we're booked [X] weeks out.
If you want to get on the schedule before [seasonal event / busy period], 
this week matters."
```

## PART 4: CTA SWIPE FILE

### CTA Principles Before the Templates

**Principle 1: The CTA tells them exactly what to do AND what happens next.**
"Click here" is not a CTA. "Click below to book your free 30-minute strategy session — we'll send you a calendar link instantly" is a CTA.

**Principle 2: The CTA language should match the offer temperature.**
A cold audience needs a low-commitment CTA ("get the free guide"). A warm audience can handle a higher-commitment CTA ("book your call"). A hot retargeting audience can handle urgency ("claim your spot before it's gone").

**Principle 3: The CTA should reduce friction, not add it.**
Tell them what to do (one action), what they'll get (the immediate outcome), and how easy it is (takes 2 minutes, instant delivery, no commitment).

**Principle 4: Match the CTA to the brand voice.**
A funny brand gets a funny CTA. A direct authority brand gets a direct CTA. A warm, empathetic brand gets a warm CTA. Never use generic language in a CTA that contradicts the tone of the rest of the ad.

Before finalizing an offer in any ad, verify:

- [ ] Is the dream outcome specific enough that a reader can picture it in vivid detail?
- [ ] Does the offer stack make the total perceived value clearly exceed the commitment?
- [ ] Is there a risk reversal that removes the biggest reason to say no?
- [ ] Does the offer have a believable reason-why if it seems too good?
- [ ] Is the CTA a single, clear action with no ambiguity about what happens next?
- [ ] Does the CTA language match the brand voice established in the rest of the ad?
- [ ] If there's urgency, is it based on something real?
- [ ] Is the offer framed in terms of the reader's outcome — not the advertiser's product features?
- [ ] Would a rational person say no to this offer? (If yes — strengthen the stack, guarantee, or specificity)

# META AD LIBRARY ANALYSIS FRAMEWORK
### Meta Ad Copywriter — Reference File
*How to systematically reverse-engineer what's winning in any market before writing a single word*

---

## HOW TO USE THIS FILE

The Meta Ad Library (facebook.com/ads/library) is the most underused research tool in paid advertising. Every ad currently running on Facebook and Instagram — including every competitor's ad — is publicly visible. For free. In real time.

Most advertisers glance at it occasionally. Elite copywriters and strategists mine it systematically before every campaign. The difference between the two is this framework.

**What the Ad Library tells you when used correctly:**
- Which angles competitors are running (and have been running longest)
- Which offers are generating enough results to keep running
- What messaging the market is already numb to — and what gap exists
- What creative formats are dominant in a niche
- What objections competitors are (and aren't) addressing
- Where the whitespace is — the angle nobody is running

**The core principle:** An ad that has been running for 3+ months without stopping is a winner. The advertiser is not spending money on a losing ad. Longevity = proof of performance. This is your most important signal.

---

## PART 1: AD LIBRARY ACCESS & SETUP

### How to Access the Meta Ad Library

**Direct URL:** facebook.com/ads/library

**No account required** — the library is publicly accessible

**Search options:**
- Search by **advertiser name** (competitor brand names, business names)
- Search by **keyword** (find ads containing specific words or phrases)
- Filter by **country**, **platform** (Facebook, Instagram, Messenger, Audience Network), **ad category**, and **date range**

**Key filters to apply for competitive research:**
- Country: set to your client's target market
- Platform: set to "All" initially, then filter to Facebook or Instagram specifically
- Status: set to "Active" first (currently running ads), then "Inactive" for historical research
- Date range: pull ads from the last 90 days for current strategy; up to 12 months for trend analysis

---

### Setting Up Your Research Session

Before opening the Ad Library, define three things:

**1. Who you're researching:**
- Direct competitors (same offer, same audience)
- Indirect competitors (same audience, different offer)
- Category leaders (the biggest spenders in the niche — they've done the most testing)

**2. What you're looking for:**
- Dominant angles (what themes appear across multiple advertisers)
- Winning hooks (what opening lines keep appearing)
- Offer structures (what they're leading with — free consultation, discount, guarantee)
- Funnel entry points (what the CTA is directing to)
- Creative formats (image, video, carousel, UGC-style)

**3. What you're NOT doing:**
- You are not copying competitor ads
- You are not looking for ideas to replicate — you are looking for gaps to exploit and patterns to understand
- The goal is to understand the market conversation, then enter it differently and better

---

## PART 2: THE 5-STEP ANALYSIS PROCESS

### Step 1 — Identify the Right Competitors to Analyze

Not all competitors are worth equal study. Prioritize in this order:

**Priority 1 — The category leader**
The brand with the most ads running, the most variety of creative, and the longest-running individual ads. They have done the most testing. Their active ads represent their best-performing angles.

**Priority 2 — The fast-growing challenger**
Brands that have dramatically increased their ad volume in the last 60–90 days. They've likely found something that works and are scaling it. Study what changed.

**Priority 3 — The direct competitor**
Businesses targeting the same customer with the same type of offer. Their angles and objection-handling are most directly relevant.

**Priority 4 — The adjacent category**
Businesses that serve the same customer with a different offer. They've solved the targeting problem. Their creative approaches may translate into your client's category.

---

### Step 2 — The Longevity Sort (The Single Most Important Signal)

When you open any advertiser's library, the first thing to do is sort by **oldest first**. This surfaces the ads that have been running the longest — which means they are performing.

**The Longevity Rule:**
- Running 0–30 days: testing phase, no conclusions
- Running 30–90 days: showing signs of performance, worth studying
- Running 90–180 days: confirmed performer — this is a winning ad
- Running 180+ days: core winner — this ad is likely generating significant ROI

**What to do:** For every competitor, identify the 3–5 ads that have been running the longest. These are their best ads. Study them in detail.

**The variation signal:** If you see 8–12 variations of an ad (same offer, different hooks or images), the advertiser is actively testing. The fact that they're running variations means the original is working and they're trying to scale it. All variations are worth studying.

---

### Step 3 — The 9-Point Ad Dissection

For each winning ad (90+ days running), complete this analysis:

**1. THE HOOK (first 1–2 lines / first 3 seconds)**
- What is the opening line?
- Is it problem-focused, benefit-focused, curiosity-driven, or story-driven?
- Who is it calling out? (explicit — "Attention homeowners" / implicit — the language signals who it's for)
- What emotion does it lead with? (fear, desire, curiosity, frustration, aspiration)

**2. THE ANGLE**
- What is the core argument or positioning?
- Is it: "we're better than alternatives," "our mechanism is different," "most people do X wrong — here's Y," "transformation story," "social proof volume," "authority"?
- What belief does this ad need to change or reinforce for the reader to take action?

**3. THE OFFER**
- What are they asking the reader to do?
- What are they giving in return?
- Is it free consultation, free resource, discount, free trial, direct purchase?
- How is it framed — low risk, high value, urgent?

**4. THE PROOF**
- What social proof appears in the copy?
- Is it: testimonials, case studies, numbers, authority/credential, before/after (where permitted), reviews?
- How specific is the proof? (vague vs. named with numbers)
- Where is the proof placed — beginning, middle, or end?

**5. THE CTA**
- What is the exact CTA language?
- What is the destination — landing page, lead form, messenger, call?
- Is there urgency or scarcity language?
- What does the CTA promise the reader will get immediately?

**6. THE FORMAT**
- Image, video, carousel, or collection?
- If video: UGC-style (person talking to camera), professional production, animation, or slideshow?
- If image: product, lifestyle, text-heavy, before/after (where compliant), infographic?
- Estimated production cost: low, medium, or high?

**7. THE AUDIENCE SIGNALS**
- Who is the copy written for? (language, references, assumed knowledge level)
- What awareness level does the copy target? (unaware, problem-aware, solution-aware, product-aware)
- Is this designed for cold traffic, warm traffic, or retargeting?

**8. THE LANDING PAGE (click the ad)**
- Does the landing page match the ad's promise?
- What is the headline of the landing page?
- Does the offer expand on the ad's offer or replicate it?
- What is asked of the visitor on the landing page?

**9. THE DURATION & SCALE SIGNAL**
- How long has this specific ad been running?
- Are there multiple variations of this ad running simultaneously? (indicates active scaling)
- Has the creative style appeared across multiple campaigns? (indicates it's a core format for this advertiser)

---

### Step 4 — Pattern Recognition Across Competitors

After analyzing 3–5 competitors and their top-performing ads, look for patterns across the entire competitive set:

**Angle Saturation Map:**
Make a simple list of every angle being used across competitors. Angles that appear in 3+ competitor ads are saturated — the market has heard them. These should be avoided or approached from a fresh direction.

Common saturated angles by niche (check these first):
- *Local services:* "licensed and insured," "family-owned," "fast response"
- *E-commerce:* "as seen on Instagram," "customers love it," percentage-off discount
- *Coaching:* "I went from broke to [number]," "the secret nobody tells you"
- *Medical:* "experienced team," "compassionate care," "accepting new patients"
- *B2B/agencies:* "proven ROI," "results-driven," "full-service agency"

**Gap Identification:**
What angles are NOT being used by any competitor? What objections are nobody addressing? What customer segment is being ignored? What proof type is absent? What offer structure is missing from the market?

This is where your client's advertising advantage lives.

**Format Dominance:**
Is one creative format (video, image, UGC, carousel) dominant across the competitive set? If yes:
- Use it (the market has trained the audience to respond to it)
- OR disrupt it (a completely different format stands out against a sea of sameness)

Disruption works best when the dominant format has become background noise. Adoption works best when the dominant format is converting and the market hasn't reached fatigue.

### Step 5 — Compile the Competitive Intelligence Brief

Produce a structured summary before writing any copy. Include:

**Market Angle Map:**
List every angle being run by competitors. Mark which are saturated (3+ competitors), which are emerging (1–2 competitors, newer ads), and which are absent (gap opportunities).

**Winning Hook Patterns:**
List the hook structures (not the specific copy) that appear in long-running ads. Example: "Problem call-out + consequence" or "Curiosity gap + timeframe claim."

**Dominant Offer Structure:**
What is the most common offer in the market? Free consultation, free trial, discount, direct sale? Understanding the market norm allows you to match it (reduce friction) or beat it (stronger offer).

**Whitespace Opportunities:**
What is nobody saying? What segment is being underserved? What objection is everyone avoiding? What format is nobody running?

**Positioning Direction:**
Based on the research, what is the single angle the client should own that no competitor has claimed? This becomes the North Star for all copy written for this client.

## PART 5: HOW TO TURN AD LIBRARY RESEARCH INTO COPY DIRECTION

### From Research to Brief: The Translation Process

After completing the Gap Analysis Template, translate findings into three specific copy directions before writing:

**Direction 1 — The Market Leader Challenger**
*If a dominant competitor is running a specific angle successfully, this direction runs the same angle but with stronger proof, a more specific offer, and a better-developed hook. The goal: out-execute the market leader on their own angle.*

Use when: the client has comparable or stronger results to competitors, and the angle is working in the market.

**Direction 2 — The Gap Angle**
*This direction runs the angle that nobody in the market is using — addressing the objection nobody handles, speaking to the segment nobody talks to, or using the proof type nobody deploys. The goal: own the conversation competitors have ignored.*

Use when: genuine whitespace exists and the client has the proof or positioning to fill it credibly.

**Direction 3 — The Format Disruptor**
*If the market is saturated with one format (e.g., all video UGC), this direction deploys a radically different format (e.g., long-form text story, documentary-style video, data-heavy infographic). The goal: pattern interrupt at the creative level.*

Use when: the dominant format has reached audience fatigue (everyone uses it, nobody responds to it the way they used to).

## PART 7: AD LIBRARY LIMITATIONS & WORKAROUNDS

### What the Ad Library Doesn't Tell You (and How to Fill the Gaps)

**It doesn't show you performance metrics.**
You cannot see click-through rates, conversion rates, ROAS, or cost per lead for competitor ads. Longevity is your proxy for performance — but a long-running ad could also be a brand awareness play with no direct response intent. Context matters: if the ad has a direct CTA and clear conversion objective, longevity = likely performance.

**It doesn't show you targeting.**
You can't see who competitors are targeting. Workaround: read the copy carefully — the language, references, and assumed knowledge level signal the audience more accurately than any targeting data would.

**It doesn't show you landing page conversion data.**
You can see the landing page, but not how it converts. Workaround: analyze how well the ad and landing page message match — strong alignment is a signal of an intentional, tested funnel. Mismatched messaging signals a gap a competitor hasn't fixed.

**It can lag on very recent launches.**
New ads sometimes take 24–48 hours to appear in the library. For real-time competitive monitoring, supplement with manual feed observation — follow competitor pages and interact with their ads to train the algorithm to show you competitor content organically.

**It doesn't show you what's been tested and failed.**
The library only shows active (or recently inactive) ads — not the full testing history. What you see are survivors. The failures (and what they tried) are invisible. This is why you should always bring fresh angles rather than only studying what's currently winning.

## HOW TO USE THIS FILE

Social proof is the most underleveraged element in Meta ad copy. Most advertisers either dump testimonials at the bottom where nobody reads them, or paste them in raw form without shaping them for conversion. Neither approach works.

This file teaches three things:

1. **The Psychology** — why specific types of proof work at specific points in the copy, and how different proof types trigger different buying decisions

3. **The Templates** — ready-to-use formats for testimonials, case studies, micro-proof, authority proof, and social proof hooks that can be adapted for any client in any niche

Specific proof ("Marcus, a gym owner in Atlanta, went from 140 to 212 members in 11 weeks") changes minds.

## PART 1: THE PSYCHOLOGY OF SOCIAL PROOF

### Why Proof Works (And Why Most People Use It Wrong)

Social proof works because of a fundamental cognitive shortcut: when people are uncertain about a decision, they look at what other people like them have done. The key phrase is *people like them.* Proof from someone who doesn't match the reader's identity, situation, or problem creates zero persuasion. Proof from someone who is uncannily similar creates immediate trust.

**The three belief barriers proof must overcome:**

1. **"Will this work at all?"** — Does the product/service produce real results for anyone?
2. **"Will this work for someone like me?"** — Does it work for my specific situation, niche, problem, or starting point?
3. **"Will this work for ME specifically?"** — Can I personally pull this off given my constraints?

Most ads only address Barrier 1. The highest-converting ads address all three — usually in that order, using progressively more specific proof as the copy moves toward the CTA.

### The 6 Types of Social Proof (Ranked by Persuasive Power for Paid Ads)

**1. Specific Client/Customer Case Study**
The most powerful. Names a real person, describes their before state, their specific result, and their timeframe. Readers self-identify with the before state and desire the after state.
*Best for: coaches, agencies, local services, high-ticket offers*

**2. Aggregated Numerical Proof**
Demonstrates volume and consistency. "Over 1,400 Atlanta homeowners have used us in the last 3 years." Numbers create social pressure and signal market validation.
*Best for: any established business with meaningful volume*

**3. Authority/Credential Proof**
Demonstrates expertise through verifiable credentials, media mentions, awards, certifications, or associations. Reduces risk for skeptical buyers.
*Best for: medical, legal, financial, B2B, coaching*

**4. User-Generated Content Style Testimonials**
Short, conversational, informal proof that reads like something someone would actually type in a review. High authenticity signal, even in written form.
*Best for: e-commerce, DTC, gyms, consumer products*

**5. Named Testimonials With Specifics**
A real person's quote, formatted with their name, location, and relevant identifier (job title, client type, situation). The name and identifier make it real. The specifics make it compelling.
*Best for: all niches*

**6. Implied Social Proof**
Social proof embedded in the narrative rather than called out explicitly. "Most of our clients come from referrals" implies that existing clients are satisfied enough to recommend. "We had to close enrollment early last month" implies demand. Works when explicit proof isn't available.
*Best for: new businesses, launches, or when explicit proof needs supplementing*

## PART 2: PROOF PLACEMENT STRATEGY

### Where Proof Lives in the Ad — By Copy Length

**The Golden Rule of Proof Placement:**
Proof never goes first and never goes last. It goes in the middle — after you've established the problem and before you make the offer. Proof placed at the end of an ad is read by almost nobody. Proof placed at the beginning (before the reader knows what the ad is about) has no context and no impact.

The exception: a proof-led hook ("My client went from 0 to $47K/month in 8 months") can work as the opening line — but only when the result is specific and surprising enough to function as a curiosity hook.

---

### SHORT-FORM AD (Under 150 words)
*Single proof element, embedded naturally*

**Structure:**
1. Hook (pain or desire)
2. ONE specific proof statement (micro-proof or short named testimonial)
3. CTA

**Where proof goes:** Line 2 or 3, immediately after the hook. It reinforces the hook's claim before the reader has time to doubt it.

**Example structure:**
```
[Hook — problem or desire]
[One specific proof statement — name, result, timeframe]
[CTA]
```

---

### MEDIUM-FORM AD (150–300 words)
*Two proof elements — one mid-copy, one pre-CTA*

**Structure:**
1. Hook
2. Problem agitation or desire amplification
3. PROOF LAYER 1 — aggregated or numerical proof ("Over 800 clients...")
4. Solution introduction
5. PROOF LAYER 2 — specific named testimonial or case study snippet
6. Offer + CTA

**Where proof goes:** Once after the problem section (proof that solutions exist), once before the CTA (proof that THIS solution works).

---

### LONG-FORM AD (300–800 words)
*Three proof elements — distributed across the arc*

**Structure:**
1. Hook (proof-led OR problem/story)
2. Problem setup and agitation
3. PROOF LAYER 1 — aggregated social proof or authority proof (builds credibility)
4. Solution and mechanism introduction
5. PROOF LAYER 2 — full case study or named testimonial (belief-building)
6. Objection handling
7. PROOF LAYER 3 — second case study OR UGC-style testimonial (objection resolution proof)
8. Offer
9. CTA

**Where proof goes:** After establishing the problem, after introducing the solution, and after handling objections. Each proof element serves a different job.

---

### RETARGETING AD (Warm Audience)
*Lead with proof — the hook IS the testimonial*

For retargeting, the reader already knows who you are and what you offer. The proof hook is the fastest way to restart the conversion conversation.

**Structure:**
1. Proof-led hook (specific case study or testimonial as the opening)
2. Brief solution reminder
3. Risk reversal
4. CTA with urgency

## PART 4: PROOF SEQUENCING BY NICHE

### How to layer proof for each client type

---

**E-COMMERCE / DTC**

Proof sequence that converts:
1. **UGC-style testimonial or proof-led hook** in the opening (high authenticity, peer-level)
2. **Numerical aggregated proof** mid-copy (volume = safety signal for skeptical buyers)
3. **Specific named testimonial** pre-CTA (person like me, got specific result)

Most important proof type: UGC and specific named testimonials. Volume proof helps.
Least effective: authority/credential proof (consumers don't care about business credentials for consumer products)

---

**LOCAL SERVICES**

Proof sequence that converts:
1. **Problem-aware hook** → transition to **aggregated local proof** early ("Over 2,300 Kennesaw homeowners have trusted us since 2017")
2. **Named testimonial with location identifier** mid-copy (neighbors trust neighbors)
3. **Objection-resolution testimonial** pre-CTA (addressing "I've been burned before")

Most important proof type: Local specificity. A testimonial from someone in their neighborhood > 500 generic 5-star reviews.
Critical add: Review platform callout ("4.9 stars on Google across 340 reviews" is powerful for local service trust)

---

**MEDICAL FACILITIES & HEALTHCARE**

Proof sequence that converts:
1. **Empathy bridge** → transition to **mini case study** of someone in the same situation (not a hook — proof comes after trust is established)
2. **Authority/credential proof** mid-copy (clinical credibility before the offer)
3. **Emotional testimonial** pre-CTA (the "I finally feel like myself again" close)

Most important proof type: Transformation stories that are emotionally resonant AND specific about the journey, not just the result.
Critical compliance note: All health testimonials must include results disclaimer. Never make the testimonial imply a guaranteed outcome.

---

**COACHES, COURSES & CONSULTANTS**

Proof sequence that converts:
1. **Proof-led hook** OR **story hook** with a proof payoff mid-narrative
2. **Mini case study** that mirrors the reader's exact before state (specificity is everything)
3. **Objection-resolution testimonial** (addresses "I've bought programs before")
4. **Aggregated numerical proof** before CTA ("847 documented client results")

Most important proof type: The mini case study that mirrors the reader's starting point exactly. If they're a gym owner stuck at $12K/month, the proof must be from a gym owner who was stuck at a similar number.
Critical add: Income/results disclaimer for business or financial coaching.

---

**AGENCIES, CONSULTANTS & B2B**

Proof sequence that converts:
1. **Specific metric-led hook** (the number has to be credible and relevant — "$0 to $31K" for a startup is different from "increased pipeline 340%")
2. **Mini case study** with business details (company type, size, situation — B2B buyers need to self-identify with the client in the case study)
3. **Authority/credential proof** mid-copy (years in market, total spend managed, notable clients if permitted)
4. **Named testimonial from a recognizable role** pre-CTA (CMO, CEO, founder — title matters in B2B)

Most important proof type: The case study with business context. "Agency owner in Chicago" is weak. "CMO of a 65-person Series A SaaS company" is strong. B2B buyers are snobs about relevance.

## PART 5: PROOF COLLECTION GUIDE

### How to help clients get the proof they need

When a client doesn't have strong testimonials or case study material, use this intake framework to gather it. These questions, asked of real clients or customers, reliably produce the raw material for compelling proof.

**The 7 Questions That Produce Powerful Testimonials:**

1. **"What was going on in your [business/life/situation] right before you started working with us / using our product?"**
*(Gets the before state in their own words)*

2. **"What had you already tried to solve this problem before you found us?"**
*(Gets the "failed alternatives" that build relatability)*

3. **"What made you hesitate before starting?"**
*(Gets the objection, which becomes an objection-resolution testimonial)*

4. **"What specifically changed or surprised you in the first [30 days / first month / first week]?"**
*(Gets the early win moment — the turning point of the story)*

5. **"What's the most specific result you can describe — a number, a date, a comparison?"**
*(Gets the specificity that makes proof credible)*

6. **"How would you describe where you are now compared to where you were when you started?"**
*(Gets the emotional transformation close)*

7. **"Who else do you think would benefit from this? What would you tell them?"**
*(Gets referral language AND reveals how they naturally describe the product/service — gold for copy)*

**Format for requesting testimonials from clients:**
Share questions 1, 4, 5, and 6 with clients. Ask them to answer in a voice message or short video for authenticity — then transcribe and lightly edit for flow. Never rewrite so heavily that the voice disappears.

## PART 6: PROOF MISTAKES TO AVOID

### The 10 Proof Mistakes That Kill Conversion

**1. Putting proof at the end.**
Nobody reads the last paragraph of an ad. Proof buried at the end doesn't convert. Move it to the middle.

**2. Using vague, unprovable testimonials.**
"Amazing product! 5 stars!" — This builds zero conviction. Every competitor has these. Replace with specifics or don't use them.

**3. Testimonials that don't match the target reader.**
A testimonial from a Fortune 500 CMO means nothing to a local service business owner, and vice versa. Match the testimonial subject to the reader's identity.

**4. Results without context.**
"Lost 30 pounds" is less compelling than "Lost 30 pounds in 14 weeks without cutting carbs, while still traveling for work." Context makes results believable.

**5. Claims that need disclaimers but don't have them.**
In health, finance, legal, and coaching, missing disclaimers on results claims is a compliance risk AND a trust risk. Add them.

**6. Fictional, composite, or exaggerated testimonials.**
Always use real, verifiable proof. Fabricated testimonials violate Meta policy, FTC guidelines, and destroy the brand when discovered.

**7. Stacking too many testimonials without copy between them.**
Three testimonials in a row reads like a brochure, not an ad. Weave proof into narrative — don't bolt it on as a block.

**8. Proof that addresses the wrong objection.**
A testimonial about how "easy the process was" doesn't help when the real objection is "will this actually work?" Identify the dominant objection and match the proof to it.

**9. Missing the emotional close.**
The most common mistake: a case study that ends with the result number and nothing else. Always close with the emotional transformation — how they feel, what's different in their life, what they can do now that they couldn't before.

**10. Proof from too long ago.**
A case study from 2018 signals to readers that you haven't produced results recently. When possible, use proof from the last 12–24 months. "Last year" and "this past spring" feel current. "Four years ago" feels like your best days are behind you.

## PART 7: COMPLIANCE RULES FOR PROOF (META + FTC)

### Non-Negotiable Rules for All Testimonials and Case Studies

**Truthfulness:** Every testimonial must be from a real person describing their genuine experience. Never fabricate, composite, or exaggerate.

**Typical results language:** If results shown are not typical, this must be disclosed. The FTC requires that testimonials reflect either typical results OR are accompanied by a clear disclosure of what typical results look like.

**Required disclaimer language (adapt by niche):**
- Health/body: *"Results not typical. Individual results will vary."*
- Income/business: *"Results are not typical and are not guaranteed. Individual success depends on effort, experience, and market conditions."*
- Medical: *"Individual results may vary. Consult your healthcare provider before beginning any program."*

**Meta-specific rules:**
- Testimonials cannot assert a personal attribute about the reader (the testimonial can say "I had diabetes" but the surrounding copy cannot say "if YOU have diabetes")
- Before/after imagery tied to weight loss, cosmetic procedures, or wrinkle treatments is prohibited — even if the testimonial is real
- Testimonials that imply a specific medical cure or guaranteed financial outcome will be flagged

**Attribution:** Named testimonials should have permission from the person to be featured in advertising. Keep signed permissions on file.

**Recency:** Testimonials more than 3–4 years old should be clearly dated or replaced with more recent proof. Undated proof from long ago that implies currency is a deceptive practice.

## HOW TO USE THIS FILE

This guide exists for one purpose: **flag compliance risks in copy before the ad is submitted, not after it gets rejected.** A rejected ad wastes time, delays campaigns, and repeated violations can restrict or permanently disable an ad account — which is catastrophic for any agency client relationship.

**Apply this file in two stages:**

1. **During copy creation** — before writing any primary text, check the client's niche against Section 3 (Niche-Specific Rules) to understand the restrictions in play.
2. **During copy review** — before finalizing any ad, run the copy through Section 4 (The Pre-Submission Compliance Checklist) to catch violations before they go live.

**Meta's enforcement is AI-driven.** The review system uses semantic pattern matching — meaning it detects the meaning and implication of language, not just specific banned words. Something can be written in a technically legal way and still get flagged if the AI interprets it as implying a prohibited thing. When in doubt, err conservative.

## SECTION 1: THE FOUR THINGS META WILL NEVER ALLOW

These are absolute prohibitions. No exception, no workaround, no grey area. If any of these appear in copy or creative, the ad will be rejected — and repeated violations risk account restriction.

### 1. Personal Attribute Assertions
Meta prohibits any ad copy that implies the advertiser knows something personal about the viewer — specifically their race, ethnicity, religion, age, sexual orientation, gender identity, disability, physical or mental health condition, financial status, voting status, or criminal record.

**The rule in plain English:** You cannot write copy that makes a reader feel like you're targeting them based on a sensitive personal characteristic.

**What this looks like in practice:**

❌ PROHIBITED — "Struggling with depression? Our program can help."
*(Implies the reader has a mental health condition)*

❌ PROHIBITED — "Are you diabetic? Try our supplement."
*(Directly asserts a health condition)*

❌ PROHIBITED — "For gay men looking to meet someone..."
*(Targets based on sexual orientation)*

❌ PROHIBITED — "Dealing with debt? You're not alone."
*(Implies knowledge of financial vulnerability)*

❌ PROHIBITED — "Are you overweight and tired of it?"
*(Asserts a physical condition)*

✅ ALLOWED — "Our program has helped thousands manage their health journey."
*(Broad reference, no personal attribute assertion)*

✅ ALLOWED — "Designed for people ready to take control of their finances."
*(Benefit-focused, no financial status implied)*

✅ ALLOWED — "If you're ready to feel better, here's where to start."
*(General desire, no condition implied)*

**The key distinction:** You can speak broadly to a desire or goal. You cannot imply you know the reader has a specific condition, characteristic, or vulnerability.

**"You" language is generally allowed** — saying "you can achieve [result]" is fine. The violation occurs when "you" is paired with a sensitive personal attribute: "you have [condition]" or "you struggle with [sensitive issue]."

---

### 2. Negative Self-Perception Tactics
Meta explicitly prohibits copy or imagery that generates negative self-perception to promote any health, wellness, weight loss, or cosmetic product or service. The goal of this rule is to prevent ads that make people feel bad about themselves in order to sell something.

**What this looks like in practice:**

❌ PROHIBITED — "Tired of looking in the mirror and hating what you see?"
*(Generates negative self-perception to sell a product)*

❌ PROHIBITED — "Your belly fat is keeping you from the life you want."
*(Body shaming to create urgency)*

❌ PROHIBITED — Copy that highlights a specific body type as "ideal" or "perfect" and implies the reader doesn't have it.

❌ PROHIBITED — Before-and-after images for weight loss, cosmetic procedures, wrinkle treatments, or body transformation. *(Specific exception: fitness classes like Pilates may use before/after. General dental products may also qualify. Verify by category.)*

✅ ALLOWED — "Feel your strongest self with a program built around how you actually live."
*(Aspiration-forward, no body shaming)*

✅ ALLOWED — "Thousands have discovered a new approach to their health goals."
*(Social proof, no negative self-image)*

✅ ALLOWED — "Our approach focuses on sustainable results — not quick fixes."
*(Benefit-focused, no body critique)*

---

### 3. Misleading, Deceptive, or Unsubstantiated Claims
Every claim in an ad must be truthful, accurate, and substantiated. This applies to:
- Product claims ("clinically proven," "FDA-approved," "guaranteed")
- Results claims ("lose 30 pounds in 30 days," "earn $10,000 this month")
- Testimonials (must reflect typical results, not outliers presented as typical)
- Comparisons to competitors (must be factually accurate)

**What this looks like in practice:**

❌ PROHIBITED — "Guaranteed to [specific result]" (unless a verifiable guarantee exists and is described accurately)

❌ PROHIBITED — "Lose [specific amount] in [specific timeframe]" (unless clinically documented)

❌ PROHIBITED — "Clinically proven" without clinical studies to substantiate it

❌ PROHIBITED — "FDA-approved" unless the product is actually FDA-approved (not just FDA-registered)

❌ PROHIBITED — Income claims ("make $5,000/month") without clear substantiation and disclaimers

❌ PROHIBITED — "As seen on [TV show/publication]" unless verifiably true

✅ ALLOWED — "Many of our clients report [result] within [timeframe]" (with appropriate softening)

✅ ALLOWED — "Our program is designed to support [goal]"

✅ ALLOWED — "Results may vary. Individual outcomes depend on [relevant factors]."

✅ ALLOWED — Specific documented case study results, clearly attributed and noted as individual results

**The safe language principle:** Use **"potential," "may," "designed to," "supports," "many clients experience"** rather than absolute guarantees or universal claims.

---

### 4. Personal Data Requests in Ad Copy or Lead Forms
Ad copy and lead ad forms cannot request sensitive personal information without Meta's prior permission.

**Copy cannot request or imply collection of:**
- Bank account or routing numbers
- Credit or debit card numbers
- Social Security or government ID numbers
- Health information (medical conditions, diagnoses, treatments)
- Financial status details (amount of debt, income, net worth)
- Insurance policy numbers

**Lead form fields cannot include:**
- "How much debt do you have?"
- "Do you have [medical condition]?"
- "What is your annual income?"
- Fields that infer sensitive personal attributes

✅ ALLOWED in lead forms — name, email, phone number, company name, business type, general interest area

- Cannot assert knowledge of the reader's legal situation ("Are you facing a DUI?")
- Cannot make guarantees about legal outcomes ("We'll win your case")
- Cannot use manipulative fear tactics that exploit a legal crisis for commercial gain
- Contingency fee language and "no fee unless you win" claims are generally allowed but must be accurate

**Safe copy approaches for legal:**
- "Have you been injured in an accident? You may have legal rights."
*(Uses "you" generically, does not assert knowledge of their specific situation)*
- "Free case review — no obligation"
- "Our team fights for the compensation you deserve"
- Avoid specific settlement amounts unless properly disclosed and attributed

**Who this affects:** Staffing agencies, recruiters, HR consultants, job boards, companies advertising open positions

**Key restrictions:**

**Special Ad Category — Employment:** Job ads, internships, professional certifications, and employment agency services require the Employment Special Ad Category designation.

**Same targeting restrictions apply:** 18–65+, all genders, no ZIP codes, no audience exclusions

**Copy restrictions:**
- No age preferences ("looking for recent graduates," "entry-level for 20-somethings")
- No gender preferences in job descriptions
- No language implying preference for or exclusion of protected classes

**Safe copy approaches for employment:**
- Focus on the role, responsibilities, and opportunity — not the ideal candidate demographics
- "We're hiring [role title]" rather than "We're looking for a [demographic description]"
- Compensation, culture, and growth opportunity are strong angles that don't risk discrimination

## SECTION 4: THE PRE-SUBMISSION COMPLIANCE CHECKLIST

*Run every piece of ad copy through this checklist before finalizing. Flag any YES answers for revision.*

### COPY CONTENT CHECK

- [ ] **Personal attributes** — Does any line imply knowledge of the reader's race, age, religion, sexual orientation, gender identity, disability, health condition, financial vulnerability, or criminal record?

- [ ] **Negative self-perception** — Does any line make the reader feel bad about their body, appearance, health, or financial situation in order to sell a product?

- [ ] **Guaranteed outcomes** — Does the copy make any absolute promise of a specific result ("you will," "guaranteed to," "100% effective") without substantiation?

- [ ] **Specific result claims** — Are specific metrics (pounds lost, dollars earned, percentage improvements) backed by documented evidence and properly softened ("many clients," "results may vary")?

- [ ] **Before/after imagery reference** — Does the copy reference before/after visual comparisons for weight loss, cosmetic procedures, or wrinkle treatments?

- [ ] **Prohibited categories** — Does the copy promote payday loans, bail bonds, tobacco, illegal products, adult content, or prescription drugs without permission?

- [ ] **Clickbait language** — Does the copy use vague "you won't believe this" language designed to mislead rather than inform about what's actually being advertised?

- [ ] **Sensational/shocking content** — Does the copy use fear, gore, crisis exploitation, or excessively disturbing content to drive action?

- [ ] **Grammar and profanity** — Is the copy free of typos, grammatical errors, and profanity? (Profanity is prohibited. Grammar errors can trigger rejection.)

---

### CLAIM SUBSTANTIATION CHECK

- [ ] **"Clinically proven"** — Is there actual clinical evidence? If not, remove or replace with "designed to support."

- [ ] **"FDA-approved"** — Is the product actually FDA-approved? If only FDA-registered, do not use "approved."

- [ ] **"#1" or "best"** — Is this claim backed by verifiable data? If not, remove or qualify.

- [ ] **"Guaranteed"** — Does a documented, honored guarantee actually exist? If not, remove.

- [ ] **Testimonial results** — Do testimonials reflect typical results, or are they outliers? If outliers, include: "Results not typical."

- [ ] **Income/earnings claims** — Are specific dollar amounts substantiated and accompanied by a results disclaimer?

- [ ] **Is this a housing, employment, or financial products ad?** → Special Ad Category designation required. Flag to media buyer.

- [ ] **Is this a health, wellness, weight loss, or cosmetic ad?** → Age target 18+, no before/after, no negative self-perception, no condition assertions. 2025 data tracking restrictions apply.

- [ ] **Is this a mental health, addiction, or eating disorder related ad?** → Apply highest sensitivity standards. No stigma, no shame, no guaranteed outcomes.

- [ ] **Is this a legal services ad?** → No outcome guarantees, no personal situation assertions.

- [ ] **Is this a supplement or wellness brand making clinical claims?** → LegitScript certification may be required as of Q4 2025. Verify with client.

## SECTION 6: WHAT HAPPENS WHEN AN AD IS REJECTED

### Immediate Steps
1. **Do not edit and resubmit immediately** — rapid resubmissions after rejection can trigger account-level scrutiny
2. **Read the rejection reason** — Meta provides a policy category in Account Quality. Use it to identify the specific violation.
3. **Review the copy against the relevant section** of this guide
4. **Revise the copy** — address the flagged element specifically, not just surface-level word changes
5. **Request a review** — use the "Request Review" option in Ads Manager or Account Quality if you believe the rejection was in error. The automated system makes mistakes.

### Account Health Awareness
Meta tracks a cumulative violation history. An account with repeated rejections will face:
- Slower ad review times
- Higher likelihood of future rejections
- Potential ad account restriction or permanent disabling

**Best practice:** Treat every rejection as a signal to audit all active and pending ads in the same client account — if one ad is flagged, similar language may exist elsewhere.

### When to Appeal vs. Rewrite
- **Appeal** when: the copy is clearly compliant and the rejection appears to be an automated false positive
- **Rewrite** when: any element of the copy could reasonably be read as a policy violation — even if you believe Meta is being overzealous, the safer path is a clean rewrite

# SECTION 3: MEDICAL FACILITIES & HEALTH SERVICES

*Covers: hospitals, urgent care centers, surgery centers, fertility clinics, weight loss clinics, mental health practices, addiction treatment centers, physical therapy, med spas, cosmetic surgery, dermatology, vision centers, hearing centers, specialized medical practices, telehealth, and any business delivering healthcare or wellness services*

---

## 3A — THE MEDICAL/HEALTH CONSUMER

### Who They Are
Someone navigating a health decision — which is among the most personal, emotional, and high-stakes decisions a person makes. They are carrying some combination of fear, hope, embarrassment, urgency, and uncertainty. They may have been living with a condition for years before seeking help, or they may be responding to an acute event. Their trust threshold is extremely high. Their desire to be understood — not just treated — is the dominant emotional need.

### Core Identity
- They want to feel seen as a person, not a diagnosis or a revenue opportunity
- They are protective of their dignity — health issues often involve vulnerability and embarrassment
- They want to be reassured that they came to the right place and made the right decision
- They want clinical excellence AND human warmth — these are not mutually exclusive in their mind

### Primary Pain Points by Subcategory

**Weight Loss / Metabolic Health Clinics:**
- Has tried every diet and every program — nothing has worked long-term
- Feels like their body is working against them (hormones, metabolism, genetics)
- Frustrated by programs that treat them like a number on a scale
- Ashamed by how many times they've "failed" — reluctant to try again
- Worried this program is just another expensive disappointment
- Wants medical guidance, not just meal plans
- Scared of being judged by healthcare providers for their weight

**Fertility Clinics:**
- Emotionally exhausted — has likely been trying for longer than they want to admit
- Scared the window is closing — urgency is real and deeply personal
- Overwhelmed by the complexity and cost of fertility treatment
- Has received conflicting information and doesn't know who to trust
- Needs hope, but calibrated hope — not false promises
- Grief is often present alongside hope — previous losses, failed cycles
- Financial stress layered on top of emotional stress

**Mental Health (Therapy, Psychiatry, Addiction Treatment):**
- Has likely been suffering in silence for longer than they should have
- Scared of being stigmatized — "what will people think if I go to therapy?"
- Has tried to "push through it" on their own and couldn't
- Worried about the cost — mental health coverage is often inadequate
- Previous bad experience with a therapist who wasn't a good fit
- Wants someone who "gets it" — not a clinical, detached professional
- For addiction: immense shame, fear of judgment, fear of failure, fear of the process

**Med Spa / Cosmetic / Aesthetic Services:**
- Wants to look like a refreshed, better version of themselves — not "done"
- Fear of looking unnatural or having results others will notice
- Embarrassed to admit they want cosmetic help — wants it to look effortless
- Has heard horror stories — worried about safety, credentials, and results
- Unsure which treatment is right for their concern — wants guidance, not upsells
- Price sensitive but willing to invest for safety and results

**Cosmetic Surgery:**
- Has thought about this for years — this is not an impulse decision
- Wants to feel like the decision is being taken seriously, not rushed
- Fears surgical risk — wants to know the surgeon is excellent and experienced
- Worried about recovery — what will they tell people? How long will they be out?
- Has done extensive research but still needs reassurance
- Wants to see results for their specific concern (age, body type, ethnicity) — not generic before/afters

**Physical Therapy / Chiropractic / Rehabilitation:**
- Living with pain that has changed their daily life — can't do things they love
- Has been told to "just rest it" or "manage the pain" — wants an actual solution
- Afraid that surgery is the only real option — wants to exhaust non-surgical options first
- Has gone to PT before without results — wonders if it's even worth trying again
- Wants to get back to their sport, hobby, or activity — that's the real goal, not just "less pain"

**Urgent Care / Primary Care:**
- Frustrated by the inability to get a same-day appointment with their regular doctor
- Anxious about severity — "Is this serious or am I overreacting?"
- Doesn't want to spend 4 hours in an ER waiting room for something that might be minor
- Wants convenient, competent care without the cost or wait of an emergency room

### Emotional Drivers (The Real Why)
- **Hope:** "I need to believe this can actually be fixed / treated / improved"
- **Safety:** "I need to know I'm in capable, qualified hands"
- **Dignity:** "I want to be treated like a person, not a case or a number"
- **Relief:** "I've been living with this for too long — I'm ready for it to stop"
- **Courage:** For many, seeking help is a brave act — the copy should honor that
- **Control:** "I want to understand my options and make an informed decision"

### Dream Outcome
The condition is resolved — or meaningfully improved. They feel better physically AND emotionally. The experience validated their decision to seek help. Their provider is someone they trust completely and will return to. They feel like themselves again — or better than they have in years.

### Common Objections
- "I don't know if my insurance covers this"

- "I don't have time for multiple appointments"

### Voice of Customer Language

- "They explained everything — I never felt confused or rushed"

- "I've tried everything. This is the first thing that actually worked."
- "It changed my relationship with [food/my body/alcohol/my mind]"
- "I feel like I got my life back"
- "The results looked completely natural — nobody suspected a thing"
- "I thought I'd have to live with this forever"
- "I only wish I had done it sooner"

### What They've Already Tried
- Primary care that offered only medication management
- Programs that worked temporarily but weren't sustainable
- Google searches that gave them more anxiety, not more clarity
- A previous provider who felt cold, dismissive, or transactional
- Waiting — living with the problem longer than they should have

### Awareness Level (Cold Traffic)
**Problem Aware to Solution Aware.** They know something is wrong or unwanted. They may not know this specific treatment, procedure, or facility exists. Rarely Unaware — health concerns tend to be top of mind.

### What Makes Them Feel Seen
- Copy that describes the specific experience of living with their condition — not just the symptoms
- Acknowledgment that seeking help took courage

- Always include appropriate disclaimers where required by the category

# SECTION 4: COACHES, CONSULTANTS & COURSE CREATORS

## 4A — THE COACHING / COURSE BUYER

### Who They Are
Someone in a state of gap — they know where they are, they know where they want to be, and they haven't been able to close that gap on their own. They are typically ambitious, self-aware, and have already consumed a significant amount of free content. They are not uneducated about the space. The problem isn't information — it's implementation, accountability, or access to a better framework. They've often bought courses or programs before that promised transformation and didn't deliver.

### Core Identity

- Knows what to do in theory but can't seem to execute consistently

**Life Coaching / Personal Development:**
- Feels stuck — knows something needs to change but can't identify what

**Fitness / Body Transformation Coaching:**
- Has started and stopped multiple times — knows the routine, can't maintain it

- Works out but eats around it — the nutrition piece is the missing link
- Has a specific event or goal (wedding, trip, health scare) creating urgency

**Relationship / Dating Coaching:**

Not just the result — the identity shift. They don't just want to make more money. They want to feel like a successful person. They don't just want to lose weight. They want to feel like an athlete. The transformation is internal first, external second. The coach's job — and the ad's job — is to sell the identity, not the tactics.

## 5A — THE B2B BUYER

### Who They Are
A decision-maker — owner, founder, CEO, CMO, VP, Director, or department head — who is responsible for a specific business outcome and is evaluating whether an outside firm or consultant can help them achieve it. - The sales process isn't documented — results depend entirely on individual personalities

## HOW TO USE THIS FILE

A hook is the first line of your primary text — the ONLY line visible before "See More." It has one job: earn the click to keep reading. If the hook fails, nothing else matters.

**The 125-character rule:** On mobile, Meta shows approximately 125 characters before truncating with "See More." Your hook must create enough tension, curiosity, or desire in that window to earn the scroll stop AND the tap.

**Hook selection logic:**
- Match the hook TYPE to the audience temperature (cold = problem/curiosity, warm = proof/outcome, retargeting = urgency/reminder)
- Match the hook EMOTION to the client's brand voice (funny brand = pattern interrupt or humor, direct brand = call-out or claim)
- Match the hook STRUCTURE to the campaign objective (lead gen = problem hooks, DTC = product/benefit hooks, coaching = story/authority hooks)

## THE 8 HOOK CATEGORIES

### CATEGORY 1 — CALL-OUT HOOKS
*Identify the reader directly by their situation, identity, or problem. The right person feels seen. The wrong person self-selects out. Both outcomes are correct.*

**By Identity:**
- If you're a [target audience] and you want [dream result]...
- If you're a [target audience] who [pain point] — this is for you.
- Attention [specific person]: if you [specific situation], read this.
- Are you a [target audience] tired of [common frustration]?
- This is for every [target audience] who has ever [relatable struggle].
- If you're between [age/stage/situation] and want [result], listen up.
- [Target audience] — if you're serious about [goal], stop scrolling.
- I am a [target audience], of course I [relatable behavior].

**By Situation:**
- If your [thing] looks like this... pay attention.
- If [specific problem] is happening in your business right now...
- If you've ever [relatable experience] and felt [emotion]...
- If you're still using [old/outdated method], this will change everything.
- If [specific trigger event] has happened to you recently...
- If you're doing [common mistake] like this — you need to see this.
- If you [action] but never get [result], here's exactly why.

**By Pain Point:**
- Going through [painful situation] and don't know what to do?
- Do you feel frustrated because you [action] and still don't get [result]?
- Tired of [specific pain point]? There's a reason it keeps happening.
- If you've tried [X], [Y], and [Z] and still aren't getting [result]...
- If [pain point] is costing you [time/money/opportunity] every single [week/month]...

## THE 5 HOOK TYPES (ORIGINAL FRAMEWORK — APPLY ALWAYS)

1. **Pattern Interrupt** — Something visually or verbally jarring that breaks the scroll rhythm
2. **Claim** — "In the next [X] seconds I'm going to show you how [specific result was achieved]"
3. **Call-Out** — Who they are / their circumstances / their problem / their desire
4. **Disqualifier** — "If you're already getting [X result], skip this. But if you're not..."
5. **Act Out the Problem** — "If you're doing [specific thing] like this — you're [making this costly mistake]"

## HOOK WRITING RULES (NON-NEGOTIABLE)

1. **The first line must work in isolation.** If it doesn't create desire or tension on its own, rewrite it.
2. **Specificity beats cleverness.** "47 HVAC owners in Atlanta" beats "local contractors" every time.
3. **Use the reader's exact language.** Mine reviews, comments, and testimonials for their phrasing — not yours.
4. **One emotion per hook.** Don't mix curiosity and fear. Pick the dominant emotion and commit.
5. **The hook must be honest.** The ad must deliver what the hook promises. Misleading hooks destroy conversion quality.
6. **Never start with "I" on cold traffic.** Cold audiences don't care about you yet. Start with them.
7. **Name the reader before you pitch.** The reader should feel called out before they feel sold to.
8. **Test the hook first.** Before testing ad body copy or creative, test 3–5 hook variations. The hook is the highest-leverage variable.
9. **Read the hook out loud.** If it sounds like an ad, rewrite it. If it sounds like something a person would actually say, keep it.
10. **125 characters.** Count them. The hook must create an open loop before the cut.

## FORMULA 1: Alex Hormozi — CLOSER Framework (Adapted for Copy)

### What It Is
Originally a sales call framework from Alex Hormozi's *$100M Leads*, the CLOSER method maps the psychological journey a buyer must take before saying yes. Adapted for ad copy, each step becomes a section of the narrative. Hormozi built his portfolio companies past $100M using this exact structure.

**C — Clarify** why they have the problem
**L — Label** the specific pain clearly and precisely
**O — Overview** of what they've already tried (and why it failed)
**S — Sell the vacation**, not the flight (outcomes, not features)
**E — Explain away** objections and concerns
**R — Reinforce** the decision and reduce buyer's remorse

### Step-by-Step for Ad Copy
1. **Clarify** — Open with a statement that names exactly why the reader is in the situation they're in. Not accusatory. Clarifying. *"Most [X] don't get [result] because they're solving the wrong problem."*
2. **Label** — Give their pain a name. A named problem feels solvable. *"That's called [specific term]. And it's costing you [specific consequence]."*
3. **Overview** — Acknowledge what they've already tried. This builds trust and shows you understand their world. *"You've probably already tried [X], [Y], and maybe even [Z]. And you're still here."*
4. **Sell the vacation** — Paint the destination vividly. Not what the product does — what life looks like after. Focus on emotional outcomes. *"Imagine waking up on Monday and already having 12 calls booked for the week."*
5. **Explain away** — Pre-empt the top 1–2 objections. Don't avoid them. Name them and diffuse them. *"You might be thinking 'this won't work for my industry.' Here's what 3 of our clients in [niche] said before they started..."*
6. **Reinforce** — Close by reaffirming the decision. Remove risk. Restate the upside. *"You've got nothing to lose and [specific outcome] to gain. Here's your next step."*

### Best Used For
- High-ticket offers ($500+)
- Lead generation (coaching, consulting, agencies, services)
- Retargeting warm audiences who've seen your content or ads before
- Any B2B or service-based client

### Meta Ad Application
- Use CLOSER for **long-form primary text** (300–800 words)
- The "Label" step makes a powerful standalone hook for the first line
- The "Sell the vacation" section becomes the emotional body of the ad
- Use "Explain away" as a mid-copy trust bridge before the CTA

### Example Hook
*"If you've tried Facebook ads before and gotten nothing, it's probably not your budget. It's your offer."*

## FORMULA 2: Alex Hormozi — The $100M Value Stack Formula

### What It Is
From *$100M Offers*, Hormozi's offer creation framework makes the value of saying YES feel mathematically undeniable. The goal: make the offer so good that saying no feels stupid. It's not about lowering the price — it's about stacking perceived value far above the price.

**Dream Outcome + Perceived Likelihood of Achievement + Speed + Effort Reduction = Irresistible Offer**

### Step-by-Step for Ad Copy
1. **State the Dream Outcome** — The #1 transformation. Specific and vivid. Not vague.
2. **Stack the components** — List what's included with real dollar value attached to each if possible
3. **Eliminate effort and time** — Show how you compress the timeline and remove the hard work from them
4. **Reduce risk** — Guarantee. Reversal. Zero-risk framing.
5. **Price anchor** — Establish what this would normally cost or what the alternative costs
6. **Make the ask** — Present the actual offer/price/CTA as the obvious, logical conclusion

### Best Used For
- Direct sales campaigns (e-commerce, courses, info products)
- Flash promotions or limited-time offers
- Lead gen where the lead magnet or free offer needs to feel premium
- Competing in a crowded market where differentiation is hard

### Meta Ad Application
- Works incredibly well as a **short-to-medium primary text** (100–250 words)
- The value stack can be formatted as a bullet list mid-ad
- Lead with the dream outcome as the hook, stack value in the body, hit the CTA hard
- Use specificity obsessively — numbers, timelines, dollar amounts beat vague claims every time

### Example Hook
*"We're basically giving away our full [product] playbook — the one that's generated $2.3M for clients — for $47. Here's everything you get..."*

## FORMULA 3: Dan Kennedy — Pain-Agitate-Solve (PAS) + The Reason Why

### What It Is
Dan Kennedy called PAS *"the most reliable sales formula ever created."* It exploits the psychological truth that people are more motivated to escape pain than to pursue pleasure. Combined with Kennedy's "Reason Why" principle — giving a specific, believable reason for your offer — it becomes one of the most versatile and powerful short-to-long copy structures in existence.

**P — Problem** — Name the pain directly
**A — Agitate** — Twist the knife. Make the pain vivid, urgent, real
**S — Solve** — Present your solution as the obvious, inevitable relief
**+ Reason Why** — Give a specific, credible reason why you're making this offer right now

### Step-by-Step for Ad Copy
1. **Problem** — State the exact problem the reader has. Be specific. Use their language, not yours. *"Most HVAC companies are paying too much for leads that never convert."*
2. **Agitate** — Describe what happens if the problem isn't solved. Add emotional weight. The problem compounds. Time passes. Money is lost. *"Every week you keep running generic ads, you're watching your competitors lock up the same neighborhoods you're trying to serve — and there's no way to get those customers back."*
3. **Solve** — Present the solution cleanly and confidently. Tie it directly to the specific pain you've been agitating. *"Our Geo-Targeted HVAC Ad System finds homeowners in your service radius within 24 hours of their unit breaking down — before they've even Googled anyone else."*
4. **Reason Why** — Give a specific, believable reason for the offer, discount, or urgency. *"We're opening 3 new markets this month and need proof-of-concept clients at reduced rate."*

### Kennedy's Copy Rules to Follow
- Write to the psyche, not the product. Speak to what they want to feel, not what you want to sell.
- Be emotionally bold. Timid copy produces timid results. "Timid salesmen have skinny kids." — Kennedy
- Write like you talk. No corporate language. No passive voice.
- Flag the reader in the headline: speak directly to a specific person in a specific situation
- Always have a hard, time-specific CTA. Deadlines are tools, not tricks.

### Best Used For
- Local services (HVAC, dental, pest control, legal, home services)
- Any cold audience that is already problem-aware but hasn't found the right solution
- Retargeting audiences who engaged but didn't convert

### Meta Ad Application
- PAS is effective at any length — it works in 3 sentences or 500 words
- The Agitate section is the most commonly underdeveloped — spend real time here
- The "Reason Why" dramatically increases credibility for cold audiences
- Use Kennedy-style directness in the headline: *"Attention [specific person]: If you [specific situation], read this."*

### Example Hook
*"Attention Atlanta dentists: If your Google ads are getting clicks but your phone isn't ringing, you're not getting bad traffic — you're sending it to the wrong page."*

## FORMULA 4: Sabri Suby — The Godfather Offer + Halo Strategy

### What It Is
From *Sell Like Crazy*, Sabri Suby's framework centers on two core ideas: (1) the **Godfather Offer** — an offer so compelling the reader feels foolish turning it down, and (2) the **Halo Strategy** — presenting massive upfront value *before* the pitch so the reader arrives pre-sold. Together they create a copy structure that leads with value, eliminates skepticism, and converts even cold traffic.

**Hook → Dream Buyer Pain → HVCO (High Value Content Offer) → Godfather Offer → Urgency/Scarcity → CTA**

### Step-by-Step for Ad Copy
1. **Pattern-interrupt hook** — Suby is famous for curiosity-gap and controversy hooks that stop the scroll cold
2. **Speak to the Dream Buyer** — Identify them precisely. Name their exact situation, frustration, or desire.
3. **Agitate with specificity** — Paint the cost of inaction in vivid, specific terms. Dollars lost, time wasted, competitors winning.
4. **Introduce the HVCO** — High Value Content Offer: a free resource, audit, guide, or result that delivers real value before asking for anything. This removes resistance.
5. **Layer the Godfather Offer** — Stack bonuses, guarantees, and proof on top of the core offer until saying no feels unreasonable. Be specific with dollar values.
6. **Urgency/Scarcity** — Make it time-sensitive or quantity-limited. Must be believable — use a specific, credible reason.
7. **CTA** — Clear, direct, friction-free. Tell them exactly what to do and what happens next.

### Suby's Core Copy Principles
- Sell the "promised land" — the vivid, emotional destination — not the vehicle (your product)
- Always assume skepticism. Build proof early. Testimonials and case studies belong in the body, not the footer.
- Headlines should do one of three things: promise a benefit, tease a secret, or call out the reader
- Use risk reversal aggressively: *"If you don't [result] in [timeframe], you pay nothing."*
- Copy length should match offer complexity. Complex/high-ticket = long copy. Low-barrier/free = short copy.

### Best Used For
- Lead generation with a free offer (free audit, free consultation, free guide)
- E-commerce with a hero product and strong social proof
- Health, fitness, and transformation offers
- Any high-skepticism category (digital marketing, supplements, finance)

### Meta Ad Application
- The hook is everything — Suby spends the most time here. Test 5–10 hook variations before scaling
- The HVCO works incredibly well as the ad's CTA: lead with the free value, not the pitch
- Use testimonials and specific case study results mid-ad, not just at the end
- Long-form copy (400–600 words) with bold sub-sections performs well in feeds for warm audiences

### Example Hook
*"The reason most dental practices never cross $1M/year has nothing to do with their service. It's a 3-word mistake they make in every ad they run. Here's what it is — and how to fix it in 20 minutes."*

## FORMULA 5: Ray Edwards — PASTOR Framework

### What It Is
Ray Edwards developed PASTOR as a deep extension of PAS, adding the critical elements of story, testimony, and offer that transform a basic problem/solution ad into a full conversion narrative. It's particularly powerful because it uses social proof and transformation stories mid-copy to do the selling, rather than relying on claims alone.

**P — Problem** — State the problem and the person who has it
**A — Amplify** — Show the cost of NOT solving it
**S — Story & Solution** — Tell a transformation story. Show the solution working.
**T — Testimony** — Provide real social proof: names, numbers, specific outcomes
**O — Offer** — Present the offer clearly and completely
**R — Response** — Tell them exactly what to do next (CTA)

### Step-by-Step for Ad Copy
1. **Problem** — Identify the reader by their problem. *"If you're a gym owner running ads and still struggling to hit 200 members..."*
2. **Amplify** — Make the cost of inaction tangible and specific. Not just emotional — economic. *"At $65/member/month, the difference between 150 and 200 members is $3,250 every single month you stay stuck."*
3. **Story** — Introduce a real or archetypal transformation story. A client who had the same problem, tried the same things, then found the solution. Write it like a movie — struggle, turning point, result.
4. **Testimony** — Drop in 1–2 real testimonials with specific results. Names and numbers add credibility. *"[Name], [City]: 'Went from 140 to 227 members in 11 weeks.'"*
5. **Offer** — Present what you're offering, what it includes, and its value. Be specific.
6. **Response** — A direct, clear, friction-free CTA. Tell them exactly what happens when they click.

### Best Used For
- Coaches, consultants, and info product creators
- Health and wellness transformation offers
- Any offer where belief is the barrier (skeptical audiences)
- Retargeting sequences where you need to convert fence-sitters

### Meta Ad Application
- The Story section is the heart — this is where metaphors and narrative structure live
- Testimonials should appear mid-copy (before the offer), not at the bottom
- Works best at 300–600 words in primary text
- Pair with a video ad where the Story section becomes the voiceover narrative

### Example Hook
*"Three years ago, Marcus was a personal trainer with 12 online clients and a prayer. Today his program does $47K/month. The difference wasn't talent. Here's what changed."*

## FORMULA 6: Russell Brunson — Epiphany Bridge / Story Selling

### What It Is
From *Expert Secrets* and *DotCom Secrets*, Brunson's Epiphany Bridge formula is built on a single insight: people don't change behavior based on logic — they change based on a shift in belief. The Epiphany Bridge is the story of the moment you (or your customer) discovered a new belief that made the solution obvious. The ad's job is to transfer that belief to the reader.

**Origin Story → The Struggle → The False Belief → The Epiphany (New Belief) → The Transformation → The Invitation**

### Step-by-Step for Ad Copy
1. **Origin Story** — Start with a relatable moment of struggle. The reader should see themselves in you. Keep it specific and human.
2. **The Struggle** — Describe what you/the customer tried before. False starts. Wasted money. Frustration. This is the "old way."
3. **The False Belief** — Name the belief that was keeping them (and the reader) stuck. *"I used to think the reason my ads weren't working was my budget..."*
4. **The Epiphany** — The moment the new belief was discovered. The turning point. Keep it dramatic but grounded. *"Then I realized it wasn't the budget at all. It was the offer."*
5. **The Transformation** — Show the result that followed the new belief. Specific outcomes.
6. **The Invitation** — Offer the reader the same shortcut/system/result. Make it a natural next step, not a pitch.

### Brunson's Key Principles
- Sell the NEW opportunity, not the improvement. People don't want to work harder at the old thing — they want a fresh start.
- The hook is the false belief, stated conversationally
- Copy should read like you're talking to a friend, not selling to a stranger
- Every ad is the beginning of a funnel — not the end. The goal is the click, not the full sale.

### Best Used For
- Courses, coaching programs, and masterminds
- Information products and online education
- High-ticket service businesses with a compelling origin story
- Any offer where the reader's false belief is the #1 barrier to conversion

### Meta Ad Application
- Works best in medium-to-long primary text (250–500 words)
- The Epiphany moment is the natural hook — lead with the new belief, then trace back to the story
- Alternatively, open with the struggle and let the epiphany be the payoff mid-copy
- Pairs extremely well with video creative where the founder tells the story to camera

### Example Hook
*"I spent $14,000 on Facebook ads in 90 days and got exactly 3 leads. I almost quit. Then I found the one thing every ad I'd ever run was missing — and it had nothing to do with targeting."*

## FORMULA 7: Donald Miller — StoryBrand (SB7) Framework

### What It Is
From *Building a StoryBrand*, Donald Miller identified that most brands make the fatal mistake of positioning themselves as the hero of the story. The customer is the hero. Your brand is the guide. Using the 7-element story structure rooted in Joseph Campbell's Hero's Journey, StoryBrand creates copy that makes the customer feel seen, understood, and led to victory.

**Character → Has a Problem → Meets a Guide → Who Gives Them a Plan → That Calls Them to Action → That Results in Success → And Helps Them Avoid Failure**

### Step-by-Step for Ad Copy
1. **Character (Customer as Hero)** — Open by naming the hero and their desire. What does your customer want more than anything?
2. **Has a Problem** — Identify the villain (the external problem), the frustration (internal problem), and the philosophical wrong (*"it shouldn't be this hard"*). The internal problem is the real motivator.
3. **Meets a Guide** — Your brand enters as the empathetic, competent guide. You've been here before. You understand. Lead with empathy, then authority.
4. **A Simple Plan** — Give them 3 clear, simple steps. Complexity is the enemy. *"Step 1: Book a call. Step 2: We build your system. Step 3: Watch leads come in."*
5. **Call to Action** — A clear, direct CTA. Don't be subtle. Be specific.
6. **Success** — Paint the "after" state. What does winning look like?
7. **Avoid Failure** — Name what's at stake if they don't act. Stakes = urgency.

### Miller's Key Copy Rules
- If you confuse, you lose. Every line of copy should be instantly clear.
- People don't buy the best product — they buy the one they understand the fastest
- Lead with empathy before authority
- The 3-step plan removes paralysis and makes the path feel safe

### Best Used For
- Local service businesses (the customer is always the hero — their home, their health, their family)
- SaaS and B2B (where clarity and simplicity beat cleverness every time)
- Brand-aware audiences who know you exist but haven't converted
- Any business with a complex or intimidating offer that needs simplifying

### Meta Ad Application
- Works best at short-to-medium length (100–250 words)
- The 3-step plan works beautifully as a mid-copy visual element or bullet list
- Lead with the character and their desire — not with your product
- The "Avoid Failure" element adds urgency without manufactured scarcity

### Example Hook
*"Running a business shouldn't mean constantly stressing about where the next client is coming from. You started this because you're great at what you do — not because you wanted to become a full-time marketer."*

## FORMULA 8: Eugene Schwartz — Awareness Level Matching (Breakthrough Advertising)

### What It Is
From *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966), Schwartz identified that the single biggest mistake in copywriting is failing to match the sophistication of your copy to the awareness level of your audience. Writing a product-aware ad to an unaware audience wastes money. Writing an unaware ad to a most-aware audience insults them. The 5 levels of awareness determine everything: the hook, the angle, the language, and the offer.

### The 5 Levels of Awareness

**Level 1 — Unaware**
The reader doesn't know they have a problem.
Copy approach: Tell a story or share a surprising fact. Don't mention your product or even the problem directly at first. Seduce them into awareness.
*Example: "The hidden reason your energy crashes at 2pm every day has nothing to do with how much sleep you're getting."*

**Level 2 — Problem Aware**
They know the problem. They don't know solutions exist.
Copy approach: Name the problem precisely. Amplify the pain. Position your solution category as the relief.
*Example: "If your lower back hurts every time you sit for more than 20 minutes, you're not alone — and it's not just age."*

**Level 3 — Solution Aware**
They know solutions exist. They haven't found the right one.
Copy approach: Lead with the unique mechanism. What makes your approach different from what they've already tried?
*Example: "Most back pain solutions treat the symptom. Ours addresses the actual root cause — spinal compression from poor hip alignment."*

**Level 4 — Product Aware**
They know your product. They haven't bought.
Copy approach: Lead with proof, testimonials, and objection handling. Give them the final push.
*Example: "Still on the fence about [Product]? Here's what 847 people said after their first 30 days."*

**Level 5 — Most Aware**
They're ready to buy. They just need the offer.
Copy approach: Lead directly with the offer, price, and CTA. Don't bury it.
*Example: "Flash Sale: 40% off ends tonight. Here's your link."*

### Best Used For
- Campaign strategy and funnel architecture (which level is this audience at?)
- Cold traffic (usually Level 1–2)
- Retargeting (usually Level 3–4)
- Email and follow-up sequences (often Level 4–5)
- Understanding why a winning ad stopped working (audience moved up the awareness ladder)

### Meta Ad Application
- Cold traffic audiences = Level 1–2 copy. Never lead with the offer to a cold audience.
- Retargeting audiences = Level 3–4. Lead with the unique mechanism or proof.
- Lookalike audiences = Level 2–3. Lead with pain or solution category.
- Always ask: *"What does this audience already know?"* before writing a single word.

## FORMULA 9: The Harmon Brothers — Comedy-Problem-Solution (CPS)

### What It Is
The Harmon Brothers produced some of the most viral direct response ads in history (*Squatty Potty*, *Purple Mattress*, *Poo~Pourri*). Their core formula uses humor and absurdity as a Trojan horse for a genuine problem/solution message. The comedy earns attention and makes the ad shareable — but the copy structure underneath is pure direct response. Crucially: the humor is never separate from the selling. The joke *is* the message.

**Absurd/Funny Premise → Real Problem Hidden Inside the Joke → "But Seriously..." Pivot → Product as Genuine Solution → Proof Delivered with Personality → Funny + Direct CTA**

### Step-by-Step for Ad Copy
1. **Absurd/Funny Premise** — Open with an unexpected, humorous scenario that is actually a metaphor for the real problem. The absurdity grabs attention; the metaphor holds the message.
2. **The Real Problem** — Make it clear, through or after the humor, that this is a real problem people have. Validate the reader's experience.
3. **"But seriously..." Pivot** — Transition from funny to direct. This is the trust-builder. *"Okay, but real talk..."* It signals: we're fun, but we're also for real.
4. **Product as Genuine Solution** — Present the product honestly and specifically. The humor has already done the hard work of building goodwill. Now the product just needs to make sense.
5. **Proof with Personality** — Use testimonials, results, and data — but deliver them in the same brand voice. Humor + proof = high credibility.
6. **Funny CTA** — Close with a call to action that matches the tone. It should be direct and funny at the same time. *"Stop Googling dumb stuff. Just click the button."*

### The Harmon Brothers' Rules
- The humor must be inherently connected to the problem/solution — not decoration
- The transition from funny to earnest is what makes it work. Never stay in the bit too long.
- Be specific with results even in a comedic context. Specificity sells even when it's funny.
- The ad should be shareable — if someone wouldn't send it to a friend, it's not doing its job

### Best Used For
- E-commerce and DTC brands with personality
- Health, wellness, and lifestyle products
- Any brand that wants to stand out in a visually saturated feed
- Products that solve embarrassing or taboo problems (the humor diffuses awkwardness)

### Meta Ad Application
- The absurd premise works as a video hook or a bold/unexpected opening line in text
- In text-only copy, the "But seriously..." pivot is the structural backbone
- Humor + specific proof mid-copy = one of the most disarming ad combinations in existence
- Pair with video for maximum impact — the Harmon Brothers formula was designed for video

### Example Hook
*"Our air filters are so good, our CEO breathed through one for 6 hours straight. His wife was not impressed. His lungs were. Here's why allergy season is about to become your most boring season ever."*

## FORMULA 10: Gary Halbert / John Carlton — The One-Legged Golfer (Specific Proof + Simple Writing System)

### What It Is
Gary Halbert and John Carlton are two of the highest-paid direct response copywriters in history. Their combined contribution to direct response copy can be distilled into two concepts: (1) Halbert's **Specific Proof Principle** — one vivid, hyper-specific claim or story beats a hundred vague claims, and (2) Carlton's **Simple Writing System** — lead with WHO the ad is for, WHAT problem they have, HOW your solution solves it, and WHY they should act NOW.

The "One-Legged Golfer" refers to Carlton's legendary ad headline: *"Amazing Secret Discovered by One-Legged Golfer Adds 50 Yards to Your Drives, Eliminates Hooks & Slices... and Can Slash Up to 10 Strokes From Your Game Almost Overnight!"* — a masterclass in specificity, intrigue, and targeted reader identification.

**Specific Hook → Reader Identification → Specific Proof Claim → Simple Explanation of How → Risk Removal → Deadline + CTA**

### Step-by-Step for Ad Copy
1. **Specific Hook (Halbert)** — Use a wildly specific, unexpected detail that creates immediate curiosity. Not "get more leads." Exactly: *"After 11 years and $3.2M in ad spend, I found the one line that doubles conversion on every ad we run."*
2. **Reader Identification (Carlton)** — In the first 2 sentences, make absolutely clear who this is for. The right reader should feel called out. The wrong reader should feel excluded. Both outcomes are good.
3. **Specific Proof Claim** — One concrete, believable, precise result. A named person, a specific number, a specific timeframe. Vague = ignored. Specific = believed.
4. **Simple Explanation** — Explain *how* the result was achieved in plain, simple language. No jargon. No complexity. Halbert called this "agora-style" — write it so an 8th grader follows it instantly.
5. **Risk Removal** — Make the decision to try it feel safe and logical. Guarantee, free trial, zero risk.
6. **Deadline + CTA (Kennedy)** — The clock is ticking. Be specific about why. Give a hard deadline. Tell them exactly what to do.

### Halbert & Carlton's Core Principles
- Specificity is the soul of credibility. One specific fact is worth ten general claims.
- The most important 5 words in any ad are the first 5 words.
- Write to one person. Not a demographic. One specific human being.
- Always answer: *"So what?"* after every line. If the line doesn't earn its place, cut it.
- Copy that sounds like a real person talking beats polished agency copy every time.

### Best Used For
- Any niche with a specific, demonstrable result (fitness, finance, business coaching, local services)
- Cold traffic who doesn't know you — specific proof builds trust fast
- High-skepticism categories where vague claims get immediately dismissed
- Breakthrough product/service launches where you need to create rapid belief

### Meta Ad Application
- The specific hook is your first line — the one visible before "See More"
- Reader identification can be embedded in the hook: *"If you run a service business and your ads get clicks but no calls..."*
- One specific case study result mid-copy is worth 3 paragraphs of explanation
- Keep the path to action simple — Carlton's rule: one offer, one CTA, one page

### Example Hook
*"In 47 days, a 58-year-old HVAC owner in Marietta went from running $3,000/month in ads with zero ROI to booking 23 jobs in a single week. He changed one thing. Here's exactly what it was."*

## AUDIENCE TEMPERATURE RULES (Apply Before Every Ad)

**Cold Traffic (never heard of you)**
- Never lead with the offer
- Lead with pain, curiosity, or story
- Build belief before you build desire
- Best formulas: PAS, Epiphany Bridge, Harmon CPS, Godfather Hook

**Warm Traffic (engaged with your content/page)**
- They know you exist — skip the intro
- Lead with proof, transformation, or the unique mechanism
- Reinforce belief and add urgency
- Best formulas: PASTOR, CLOSER, Value Stack, Specific Proof

**Hot Traffic / Retargeting (visited your page, watched your video, clicked your ad)**
- They've seen your offer — now remove the final objection
- Lead with risk reversal, urgency, or a simplified offer restatement
- Best formulas: CLOSER (E + R steps), Awareness Level 4–5, Godfather Offer reinforcement
